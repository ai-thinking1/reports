1. Framing — Sanyal's thesis, market theory, and the honest baseline

This report sits on top of three frames the user explicitly asked for. Each cuts the data differently, and you need all three to read what's actually happening.

Frame 1: Sanyal's "democratization of expertise" thesis

Sanjeev Sanyal, in a thread carried by Swarajya on 12 May 2026, argued that AI — like firearms before archers and English mills before Indian weaver guilds — will democratize expensive expertise rather than concentrate it. A citizen drafts a tenancy contract without a lawyer. An amateur scriptwriter competes with a famous director. A junior engineer prints a part that once needed advanced forging. Policy implication: young people should enter the workforce sooner; education should be lifelong, iterative, in short bursts across several careers.

The thesis is half-right: the technology pattern is real. But the historical analogies skip what happened to the people displaced. The English peasant-turned-musketeer became cannon fodder for centralized state armies. The Indian weaver got de-industrialization, not new factories — those came 80 years later. Democratization of production has historically not democratized prosperity for the workers displaced. Whether AI breaks this pattern is an empirically open question that depends on capital accumulation pace, redistribution policy, and whether new tasks emerge faster than old ones are automated.

Frame 2: Efficient market theory

Markets aggregate information and price assets accordingly. Applied to labor and tech:

  • Wages signal future demand. The CS salary boom of 2010-2022 told young people to study CS. The flattening of new-grad CS wages in 2024-25 is now sending a different signal.
  • Capital flows are forward-looking. Magnificent-7 valuations imply huge expected productivity gains from AI; if those don't materialize, you'd expect a correction. Capex of $300B in 2025 and projected $500B+ in 2026 from hyperscalers is the market betting heavily on the productivity case.
  • EMH violations matter here. Workers cannot price 10-year automation risk well (information asymmetry). Retraining has slow adjustment lags. Career choice has coordination failures — everyone goes into CS at once, creating oversupply. Behavioral biases (recency, overconfidence) push young people into whatever was hot 5 years ago. So while markets price assets efficiently in seconds, labor markets adjust over a generation. This gap is where Sanyal's "earlier entry, lifelong learning" advice has practical bite.
  • Don't confuse markets with prophecies. Markets price what allocators believe today. The dotcom bubble priced "everyone will buy pet food online by 2002." That belief was wrong. Current AI capex may be similarly mispriced — or correctly priced. We won't know until we know.

Frame 3: An honest baseline on what current AI actually is

The user's framing here is correct and important to state plainly:

Current AI (Claude, GPT, Gemini, etc.) is a productivity tool, not autonomous labor. It excels at white-collar tasks involving language, code, image manipulation, and structured reasoning. It cannot independently run a business, perform surgery, install plumbing, drive a truck, or build a chair. Even its white-collar productivity gains depend on a human reviewing, prompting, and correcting.

The job risk is therefore not "AI replaces you." It's "AI lets one person do three people's work, so the firm needs one-third as many people." Same end result, different mechanism. Frames in the rest of this report stick to this realism — there is no "prompt engineer" career path treated as a real profession.

Frame 4: The whole tech stack is moving at once

AI is the loudest wave but not the only one. Solar costs have fallen 99.6% since 1977. Battery costs fell 93% since 2010. DNA sequencing costs fell 5+ orders of magnitude in 20 years. LEDs, electric motors, additive manufacturing, mRNA platforms, drones, satellite communication, and (more speculatively) fusion and quantum are all moving down learning curves simultaneously. Several of these create real new job categories — solar installers, battery technicians, EV mechanics, grid engineers — that are not "AI-related" and are not exposed to AI automation in the near term. Section 9 of this report covers the full stack.

2. Long-run structural shifts

The first thing to understand about the AI moment is that we've been here before. Three times. The agricultural workforce shrank from ~70% to ~2% in advanced economies between 1850 and 2000. The manufacturing workforce rose, peaked, and is now falling. Services dominate. The question for AI is whether it accelerates the next phase or breaks the pattern.

US employment share by sector, 1840–2020

The canonical structural-transformation template. Agriculture collapsed, manufacturing rose and fell, services took over.

Agriculture Manufacturing Services & trade Construction & other industry

Sources: US Census long-run series (Weiss; Lebergott); BLS; IPUMS OCC1950. Pre-1900 figures are estimates; post-1950 from CPS/CES.

Agriculture's share fell from 65% (1840) to 1.4% (2020) — about six generations of farming families finding other work. The transition wasn't smooth: every decade, several percent of the population had to switch industries. The mechanism wasn't "agriculture disappeared" — output rose dramatically — it was that productivity gains let fewer people produce more.

Manufacturing followed the same arc with a lag: it rose from 14% (1840) to peak ~32% in 1953 and has been falling for 70 years. Services absorbed the displaced. The wave you're seeing today with AI hitting services is the third movement of the same symphony — except services has nowhere to displace to, which is the open question of the next 30 years.

Two things jump out from India's chart that the US chart doesn't have:

  1. Manufacturing never had a hump. It's been flat at 9-12% for 70 years. India never went through the China-style absorption of agricultural labor into factories. The manufacturing transformation that the US did between 1900 and 1950, and that China did between 1980 and 2010, India has not done.
  2. Agriculture went back up after 2018. The post-COVID reversal. Construction (grey) is now doing the absorption work manufacturing was supposed to do, but construction is cyclical, informal, and low-productivity. It's a sponge, not a ladder.

Manufacturing's share of employment by country, 1950–2024

Who industrialized, who's deindustrializing, who never did.

USA UK Germany Japan China India

Three patterns:

  • Anglo-American deindustrialization: US and UK both peaked around the same era, both crashed to 8%. UK started higher (workshop of the world) and fell harder.
  • German and Japanese persistence: Both held 16–18%. Their export-oriented quality-goods model preserved manufacturing employment. This is the "Mittelstand" pattern.
  • China's hump is just starting to descend. Already past peak. As China ages and automates, its manufacturing share is now declining — which is what creates the "China + 1" supply chain opportunity for India and Vietnam, if they can take it.
  • India's flat line. The thing that never happened. India is the only major economy where manufacturing share has not had a sustained rising phase, despite seven decades of independence and 30+ years of liberalization.

3. The hot profession through history — engineering succession

Your intuition that "before software it was electrical engineering, before that mechanical" was directionally right — but the order is slightly different and the waves overlap heavily. There's no single "current hot profession" replacing the previous one. Each new wave layers on top.

The hot engineering discipline, by era (1820–2030)

Each bar shows when the discipline had its window of fastest hiring growth and wage premium — not when it existed. They overlap.

A few corrections to the popular telling:

  • Civil engineering was first. The 1820s–1900s wave. The very word "engineer" meant "civil engineer" outside the military for most of the 19th century. Canals, railways, bridges, urban water and sewage. The Brunels, Telfords, and Roeblings.
  • Mechanical was the long incumbent. From 1860s through to mid-20th century, ME was the largest engineering profession by headcount and the highest-status. Steam, machine tools, automotive. Still the largest engineering profession in many countries today by total employment.
  • Electrical had the longest reign. 1890s through 1970s. It kept absorbing new sub-fields (power, lighting, motors, telephony, radio, semiconductors, computers) so the "hot phase" of EE lasted nearly a century. Computer engineering started as a sub-department of EE in the 1960s.
  • Chemical and aerospace were post-war additions. Petrochemicals, plastics, pharmaceuticals on one side; aviation and the space race on the other. Smaller in absolute terms than ME/EE but very high wage premium during their peak.
  • Computer/software is the current incumbent. It started in the 1960s but the wage and hiring boom really kicked in around 1995. We're now about 30 years into a roughly 50-year wave.
  • AI/ML is not a new wave. It's a sub-specialty within software, the way semiconductor design was inside EE. The "prompt engineer" job title is a temporary skill, not a profession. Same with "data scientist" — increasingly absorbed back into software engineering as ML tools commoditize.

What's actually next? Honest answer: we don't know yet. Three candidates each have the pattern of a discipline waiting for its hardware to mature:

  • Robotics — the strongest candidate. The combination of cheap compute (AI vision/control) + cheap actuators + cheap batteries makes general-purpose robots feasible for the first time. If humanoid robots hit $15-20K per unit by 2030, this becomes the discipline that absorbs ME/EE/CS specialists into a unified field.
  • Energy systems — power grids becoming software-defined, battery-driven, distributed. New jobs in grid engineering, virtual power plants, microgrids, battery design, fusion (if it works).
  • Bioengineering — has been "the next big thing" since the 1990s. It will be the wave if and only if biology has a "transistor moment" — a small reliable building block that lets engineers compose systems. CRISPR was a step. mRNA was a step. We're not there yet but closer.

4. Where jobs are growing and dying, 2025–2030

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 surveyed 1,000+ employers covering 14M workers across 22 industry clusters. The headline: 92M jobs displaced, 170M created, net +78M globally by 2030. That's 22% structural churn across the 1.2B formal jobs studied.

Net change in millions of workers. Frontline and care jobs grow most; clerical declines hardest.

Growing Declining

Source: WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025 (Jan 2025).

This data breaks the old "low-skill out, high-skill in" frame. Notice:

  • The growth list is frontline manual + care + frontier tech. Farmworkers, delivery drivers, construction, nurses, care workers — and software developers, big data specialists, renewable energy engineers.
  • The decline list is clerical middle. Accountants, bank tellers, insurance underwriters, administrative assistants, data entry, cashiers. These were the "respectable middle-class jobs" of the 20th century. They're the ones being eliminated.
  • This is the opposite of the 1980s-2010s pattern, where automation killed manual/routine work and grew the cognitive middle. The current wave hollows out the cognitive middle while leaving frontline manual largely alone (until robotics catches up — see section 6).

Two observations:

  • The orange "augment" column is dominant for most white-collar work. This matches the user's frame: today's AI is a productivity tool, not autonomous labor. A lawyer with AI is faster, not absent. A doctor with AI catches more cases, not fewer.
  • But economic effects flow through "augment" too. If AI lets a lawyer process 3x more cases, the law firm might need 1/3 fewer lawyers — even though AI is not "replacing" anyone. The mechanism is productivity compression, not direct substitution.
  • Manual frontline work is largely untouched by current AI. Plumbers, electricians, nurses, farmworkers, construction. This is the robotics question, not the AI question.

The four-quadrant reading:

  • Top-left (robotics, AI-safe): Truck drivers, warehouse, factory line, assembly. Huge global headcount — this is where 100s of millions sit. Currently safe from AI but on the front line of the humanoid robotics wave starting ~2028–2032.

  • Bottom-left (safest, for now): Skilled trades (electricians, plumbers, barbers), care work (nurses, care workers), farmworkers. Note that farmworkers are the largest single category globally (~900M including subsistence farming).

The data confirms Sanyal's analogy at the production level. India did lose 90%+ of its global industrial share to mechanized British competition. But the data also exposes what his framing skips:

The displaced Indian weavers were not absorbed into Indian factories. Bombay's first textile mill opened in 1854 — three generations after the collapse began. The displaced weavers became agricultural labour. India's industrial recovery took until the late 19th century to begin in any serious way, and didn't reach pre-colonial scale until well after independence. The "English peasants ran the new mills" half of the analogy was true in England. The Indian half of the analogy was a multi-generational catastrophe.

Read across to AI: democratization of expertise happens where the AI deployment occurs. If the deployment happens in California cloud regions and the displaced workforce is in Bengaluru BPO, the displaced workforce doesn't get the new jobs unless India deliberately builds the deployment locally. This is the central policy question for India's IT services sector right now.

9. The convergent technology stack — beyond AI

This is the section the first version of this report missed. AI is loud but it's only one of perhaps six transformative cost-decline curves running simultaneously. Some of these create jobs that AI doesn't touch.

Cost decline curves: six transformative technologies, log scale

Each line is a different unit of measurement, normalized so you can see the shape. Solar fell 99.6% since 1977; batteries 93% since 2010; sequencing 5+ orders of magnitude in 20 years.

Solar PV ($/W) Lithium battery ($/kWh) DNA sequencing ($/genome) AI compute ($/MFLOP-s) LED lighting ($/klm) Wind turbine ($/kW)

Sources: Solar (Swanson's Law / IRENA / Nemet 2009); Battery (BloombergNEF 2010–2025); Sequencing (NIH/NHGRI); Compute (Mehta & Hobbhahn 2024, Epoch AI); LED (DOE); Wind (IRENA). Log scale; each value normalized to its 1977/2010/2003 starting point.

All six lines follow some version of Wright's Law: cost declines by a fixed % for every doubling of cumulative production. Solar drops 20% per doubling (Swanson's Law). Batteries ~19%. Wind ~15%. LEDs ~28%. The decline is mechanical — driven by manufacturing learning, not by any policy breakthrough — and it does not stop unless something blocks volume growth.

The implication: multiple "AI-equivalent" transformations are happening in parallel. Cheap solar plus cheap batteries plus cheap power electronics is electrifying transportation, heating, and industry. Cheap sequencing plus cheap synthesis is making programmable biology feasible. Cheap satellite launch is making global connectivity a commodity. Each of these creates job categories that AI does not directly threaten — installation, maintenance, system integration, application engineering.

Adoption S-curves: where each technology sits on its diffusion curve

% global penetration of relevant market. Some are mature (LED, smartphones); some in steep middle (solar, EVs); some early (humanoid robotics, gene therapy).

Compiled from IEA, BNEF, IRENA, IDTechEx, FDA, OEC; "% penetration" definition varies by tech and is indicative not strict. AI assistant adoption based on Pew / Statista user surveys.

Reading this chart is more art than science — the y-axis is "% of relevant market penetrated" and "relevant market" is defined differently for each. But the shape is what matters. Most transformative technologies follow an S-curve: slow start, fast middle, plateau at the top. The middle, where adoption goes from ~10% to ~80%, takes typically 15–25 years.

Where each technology sits today (mid-2026):

  • Mature / plateau: LED lighting (~80% of new bulbs), smartphones (~85% of adults globally), 4G/5G mobile (~75% coverage).
  • Steep-middle: Solar PV (~7% global electricity, growing fast), EVs (~22% of new car sales globally), AI assistants (~30% of knowledge workers using regularly), battery storage (~3% but accelerating).
  • Early adoption: Humanoid robotics (~0.001% of relevant labour), CRISPR/gene therapy (~10 approved therapies, expanding), autonomous vehicles (commercial deployment in a few cities), generative AI agents (very early enterprise integration).
  • Pre-adoption / R&D: Commercial fusion, room-temperature superconductors, full general AGI, Neuralink-class BCIs, lab-grown meat at price parity.

The crypto question. The user asked specifically about crypto. Honest read: crypto is real as financial infrastructure (stablecoins are now ~$200B+ in circulation and growing) but its direct labour-market impact is small. Total blockchain/crypto employment globally is estimated at 400-500K, vs ~30M software developers overall — i.e., crypto is roughly 1-2% of software jobs. Where it matters more is as remittance and savings infrastructure for emerging markets including India: lower-cost cross-border payments could lift wages effectively for the ~13M Indian diaspora sending ~$120B home annually. So crypto belongs in the report as financial plumbing, not as a major employment category.

10. Efficient-market theory and what capital is already pricing in

If markets are even partly efficient, current capital flows tell us what informed allocators believe about the future of work. Three signals:

Where capital is flowing: market cap and VC allocation by sector, 2025

Public market cap concentrated in AI-adjacent tech; VC flows show where the next wave is being bet on.

Public market cap: S&P 500 sector weights (Dec 2025) and Magnificent 7 share; VC: PitchBook Q1 2026 by deep-tech category. Indicative shares.

What the chart says:

  • The "Magnificent 7" alone are ~32% of S&P 500 market cap. Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Tesla. The market is making a very large bet that AI/cloud productivity gains will accrue to these specific firms. If even half-right, that's a forecast of enormous capital-share-of-GDP shift — and a corresponding wage compression for cognitive workers whose labour those firms substitute.
  • VC is more diversified. AI is biggest, but climate/energy (solar, batteries, storage), biotech (mRNA platforms, AI-discovered drugs), defense tech, and space are all substantial. VC's pattern is closer to "place bets on the full convergent stack" than "AI über alles."
  • What the market is not pricing well: distributional outcomes. Stock prices reflect expected firm profits, not expected wages. The same firm that has rising profits via labour substitution will have rising stock prices and falling wages simultaneously. Markets price this correctly for shareholders and badly for workers.

What's actually happened to wages — the market's realised signal, not a forecast:

  • Software engineer premium has plateaued or fallen slightly (3.4x → 3.2x for new grads at average firms). Top FAANG/AI lab compensation is rising fast, but the median is not.
  • Skilled trades (electricians, plumbers) premium has risen — labour shortage in trades plus rising hourly wages.
  • Bookkeepers, paralegals, customer service — flat to falling premium. Market pricing in displacement.
  • Nursing premium has risen — aging population demand exceeding supply.
  • Solar/wind technicians didn't exist as a category at scale in 2010; by 2025 they have a real wage premium and are the fastest-growing BLS occupation category (projected +60% from 2023 to 2033).

The Sanyal frame, market-tested. If Sanyal's "democratization of expertise" thesis is right, we should see white-collar premium compress (which is happening for clerical/legal but not yet for top-end software) and skilled-trade premium rise (which is clearly happening). On both counts, the labour market is already partway through pricing in this thesis. The question is whether the compression continues to top-end professional work or stops at the bookkeeper level.

What this means for India

India's specific opportunity within the convergent stack:

  • Solar manufacturing & deployment: India is now the world's 3rd-largest solar installer. By 2030, ~500K solar installer + technician jobs are plausible (vs ~20K in 2020).
  • EV/battery manufacturing: PLI scheme + Indian auto base could create 1-2M jobs in 2-wheeler EV manufacturing, battery cell production, and EV repair by 2030.
  • Pharma / biomanufacturing: India makes ~20% of world's generic drugs. Biosimilars, vaccines, biologics are the next layer.
  • Skilled trades for electrification: Massive shortage of qualified electricians for rooftop solar, EV charging, smart grid. ITI revival as a key policy lever.
  • Climate adaptation construction: Coastal infrastructure, water systems, cooling — these jobs grow with warming.

The catch is that India needs to build the deployment domestically. If Chinese-made solar panels are installed by Indian workers, India gets the install jobs (~10% of total value). If Indian-made panels are installed by Indian workers, India gets ~70% of value. PLI is targeting this gap.

12. India scenario: combined effects through 2050

Pulling all of the above into a single forward view for India.

India sectoral employment, observed 2000–2024 + three scenarios to 2050

Stacked share of employment. Scenarios diverge sharply after 2030 depending on policy execution.

Observed: PLFS, NSSO. Projections: author scenario modeling combining IMF/World Bank baseline with NITI Aayog Vision 2047 sector targets.

The three scenarios:

Scenario A — "Productivity Dividend" (Sanyal-optimistic)

AI democratization works as Sanyal hopes. Indian engineers, agronomists, doctors leverage AI to serve global clients at scale. PLI + China+1 takes manufacturing share from 11% → 18%. FLFP rises to 45% through AI-augmented flexible work. Female-headed micro-enterprises proliferate via vernacular AI tools.

Requirements: solve rural digital divide (BharatNet completion), formalize informal sector (e-Shram → portable benefits), avoid caste bias in AI hiring, deliberate domestic AI deployment (so jobs are in India, not for India's clients).

Outcome: per-capita GDP ~$15K by 2050. Manufacturing 18%, services 38%, agriculture 25%, construction 15%, mining/utilities 4%.

Scenario B — "Middle-Income Trap with White-Collar Bypass" (base case)

IT services growth flattens; BPO doesn't absorb the next generation. Manufacturing stays at ~12-13% (Vietnam absorbed China+1 better). Construction and informal services keep absorbing displaced labour at low productivity. Demographic dividend partially wasted.

Outcome: per-capita GDP ~$8-10K by 2050 (vs South Korea's ~$30K+). India ages before it gets rich.

This is roughly the IMF/World Bank base case if structural reforms don't accelerate.

Scenario C — "Disruption Cascade" (tail risk)

AI displaces IT/BPO/GCC employment 30-40% by 2035. Humanoid robotics at $15K/unit by 2032 erodes manufacturing labour cost arbitrage. China + Vietnam + Bangladesh absorb manufacturing through automation. Youth unemployment rises sharply.

Sectoral polarization: 30M-strong urban professional/tech class lifts off; the 500M+ informal mass stays stuck. Political pressure for welfare expansion strains fiscal balance.

Outcome: per-capita GDP ~$6K by 2050. Major social tension. Lost decade.

Most likely actual outcome: a regional mix. Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Andhra, Kerala, Maharashtra likely trace Scenario A. The Hindi heartland (UP, Bihar, MP, Rajasthan, Jharkhand) is at higher risk of B/C. This is the long-running North-South divergence on full display, with AI/automation likely to widen it.

13. Bottom line

Pulling all of the above together — historical patterns, current data, the convergent tech stack, market signals — here's the honest read:

  1. This is the third great structural transformation. Agriculture → manufacturing → services took 200 years and was wrenching every time. AI-driven services compression is the same kind of event, compressed to 20-30 years.
  2. The engineering succession the user intuited is real but layered. Civil → mechanical → electrical → chemical/aerospace → computer/software. Each wave layers on top, doesn't replace. The next wave is probably robotics + energy systems + (eventually) bioengineering. AI itself is not a new wave — it's a tool used across the existing waves.
  3. Current AI is a productivity tool, not autonomous labour. The user is right to be sceptical of "prompt engineer" hype. Real jobs from AI come from deployment (data engineering, ML ops, AI safety, evaluation, integration) and from new product categories (AI-augmented healthcare diagnostics, education tutoring, agricultural advisory) — not from "AI specialist" as a stand-alone profession.
  4. The convergent tech stack matters as much as AI. Solar (-99.6%), batteries (-93%), sequencing (-99.99%), wind, LEDs are all moving down learning curves simultaneously. Together they create real new job categories — installers, technicians, biomanufacturing operators — that AI does not directly threaten. These are the jobs India should prepare for.
  5. Markets are already pricing in the shift. Mag 7 valuations imply huge expected capital-share gains from AI. Wage premiums for clerical work are flat to falling. Trade premiums are rising. Markets are partly right; the question is whether they're also right about the timing.
  6. India's exposure is asymmetric and time-limited. Most of India's workforce (agriculture, construction, manual services) is currently insulated from AI. The economically critical IT/services class is highly exposed. India has until ~2035-2040 (peak demographic dividend) to capture the manufacturing-plus-frontier-services wave before automation makes labour cost arbitrage moot.
  7. Sanyal is half-right and importantly so. Yes, AI democratizes access to expensive expertise — that's the firearms-vs-archers part. No, that historically did not democratize prosperity for the displaced — that's the part the analogy hides. The English peasants who picked up muskets became cannon fodder. The Indian weavers became agricultural labour. Whether AI breaks this pattern depends on policy choices being made now, not on the technology itself.
  8. What young people should actually do. The honest advice: (a) get a broad foundation in math, communication, and one technical domain; (b) develop skill in physical or relational work that AI can't do, or in building things on top of AI rather than competing with it; (c) treat the first job as temporary; (d) optimize for learning and optionality rather than salary at age 22; (e) for India specifically, watch the renewable energy, manufacturing, and skilled-trades sectors as carefully as IT.
