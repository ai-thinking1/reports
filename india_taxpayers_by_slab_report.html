Chapter 01

The structure: six brackets, two regimes.

Since FY 2023-24 the new tax regime has been the default for individuals, HUFs, AOPs and BOIs. Taxpayers can opt out and pay under the older slab structure, which trades higher rates for the right to claim deductions. Here are the actual rates a taxpayer faces in each regime — for the most recent two financial years.

The structural difference

Under the old regime, a salaried person earning ₹12 lakh paid roughly ₹1.72 lakh in tax (before 80C). Under the new regime at FY 25-26 rates, the same person pays zero — provided they don't need to claim large deductions. The reform is essentially a swap: lower rates in exchange for giving up shelter.

Chapters 02 through 07 — the 8.69 crore individuals who file

Chapter 02

Where individuals sit in the slab.

Of every 100 individual returns filed, roughly 54 declare gross total income under ₹5 lakh (many below the taxable limit but filing for refunds or documentation), 24 in the ₹5–10 lakh bracket, 13 in ₹10–25 lakh, and the rest spread across the higher brackets.

Reading the bottom rung

About 3.47 crore of the 6.37 crore individual filers in AY 2021-22 reported gross income under ₹5 lakh. A large fraction sit below the taxable threshold entirely — they file to claim TDS refunds, to show income for visas or loans, or because they run small businesses where filing is a compliance habit.

Chapter 03

The middle brackets quadrupled.

CBDT's longitudinal cut from AY 2013-14 to AY 2021-22 reveals the most striking shift in Indian taxpayer composition: filers in the ₹5–10 lakh and ₹10–25 lakh brackets grew nearly 4×. The bottom bracket grew only 32%. The shape of the pyramid is changing.

A counter-intuitive distribution finding

The average gross income of the bottom 25% of individual taxpayers grew 58% — versus 42% for the top 1%. The share of all individual income held by the top 1% actually fell from 15.9% to 14.6%. The middle 74% saw their share rise from 75.8% to 77%.

Chapter 04

Salaried vs self-earned.

Of all individual taxpayers, the CBDT splits them into two streams: those reporting income primarily from salary and those whose income comes from business, profession, or other sources — which includes freelancers and contractors. The salaried side grew faster.

Chapter 05

By the form they file.

The ITR form chosen is the cleanest signal of who a taxpayer is. ITR-1 (Sahaj) is for salaried with income ≤ ₹50 lakh. ITR-2 covers individuals with capital gains or multiple house properties. ITR-3 is for business and professional income (full books). ITR-4 (Sugam) is the presumptive scheme — where most freelancers, contractors and small businesses sit.

The freelancer / contractor signal

1.88 crore taxpayers filed ITR-4 (Sugam) in AY 2024-25 — over a quarter of all returns. This is the cleanest proxy India has for its freelancer, gig worker, contractor and small-business population that opts into the presumptive scheme. Add ITR-3 (91 lakh non-presumptive business filers) and the entrepreneurial cohort approaches 2.8 crore.

Chapter 06

Old regime to new, in two years.

When the new regime became default for AY 2024-25 via the Finance Act 2023, the migration was swift. Of the 7.28 crore returns filed by 31 July 2024, 5.27 crore (72%) were under the new regime — and the CBDT chairman has said this should reach over 90% once the FY 2025-26 ₹12-lakh zero-tax threshold filters through.

Chapter 07

At the top: the crorepati taxpayer.

One of the cleanest signals of Indian formalisation is the count of individuals declaring annual gross total income above ₹1 crore. That number was just 44,078 in AY 2013-14. By AY 2024-25 it had crossed 3.3 lakh — a 7.4× expansion in twelve years.

A telling tail

Within the crorepati category, in AY 2022-23, just 1,798 individuals declared income above ₹25 crore — down marginally from 1,812 a year earlier. And only 1,577 salaried individuals sit above ₹10 crore. The ultra-tail is small and remarkably stable; the broad expansion is happening in the ₹1-5 crore band.

Chapter 08

Companies in their own frame.

About this report

Companion to Report I (The Indian Taxpayer — A Decade in Data), this volume focuses on the within-base breakdown: by income slab, by tax regime, by ITR form, and by entity category. Individuals are kept visually and structurally separate from companies and other entities throughout.

Slab rates reflect the Finance Act 2023, Finance Act 2024 and Finance Act 2025 amendments (the last raising the new-regime zero-tax threshold to ₹12 lakh effective FY 2025-26).
