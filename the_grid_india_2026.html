01 / OVERVIEW

The picture, straight.

India built its first 100 GW of installed capacity in 60 years. It built the most recent 100 GW in roughly seven. The grid now serves 100% of villages and roughly 1.4 billion people. It also faces the hardest operational challenges in its history: record peak demand, a heatwave-sensitive load profile, a duck curve from too much midday solar, and distribution companies still leaking money. Every problem in this report is a problem of scale and speed.

02 / THE PARADOX

Too much sun at noon. Not enough at night.

The single most discussed operational problem on the Indian grid right now is the duck curve — and yes, your intuition about it is correct. Solar generates abundantly between 10 AM and 4 PM. Demand peaks between 6 PM and 10 PM, just as solar collapses to zero. The gap has to be filled by something dispatchable — coal, gas, hydro, or batteries — that can ramp up fast. As more solar is added, the "belly" of the duck gets deeper and the evening "neck" gets steeper.

The visible cost is solar curtailment — wasted clean electricity that the grid cannot absorb. India saw over 2 TWh of solar curtailment in 2025, with high-producing states like Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu forced to physically halt generation during midday surpluses because the grid had nowhere to put the power. The invisible cost is harder: coal and gas plants are forced to cycle up and down sharply (the "evening ramp"), which accelerates wear, raises maintenance costs, and makes those plants more expensive even as they run fewer hours.

On record-demand day April 25, 2026, when load hit 256 GW, solar supplied about 58 GW at midday (~24% of peak). But solar's contribution after sunset was effectively zero. The coal fleet did the evening work. This is the structural reason why even hitting 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030 does not, by itself, solve India's electricity problem.

03 / THE COOLING SURGE

India is buying air conditioners.

The chart above ends in the evening peak. The reason that peak keeps rising — and is increasingly the binding constraint on the whole system — is one appliance: the air conditioner. The IEA's 2025 analysis is blunt about it: each additional degree of daily average temperature now adds more than 7 GW of peak demand, twice the sensitivity in 2019. By 2027, that figure could exceed 11 GW per degree.

The Berkeley study is the policy reason why you are increasingly hearing about mandatory minimum AC temperature settings. The government is actively considering capping AC settings between 20°C and 28°C — a measure that, according to the study, could shave up to 60 GW off peak demand by 2035 and avoid roughly ₹7.5 lakh crore of new generation and grid investment. Whether or not the proposal is mandated, every degree higher on every thermostat in India is the single cheapest "power plant" the country can build.

Even in winter, peak load now crosses 245 GW — January 2026's peak rivalled summer highs. Cold-wave days in northern India in December 2025 set new winter records. India's load curve is no longer a summer-only story.

04 / ROOFTOP REALITY CHECK

PM Surya Ghar: the gap between sign-up and sunlight.

In February 2024 the government launched PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana — a ₹75,021 crore scheme to put rooftop solar on 1 crore households by March 2027, with subsidies up to ₹78,000 per home. Adoption has been strong on paper. Implementation has been bumpy on the ground, and three specific problems keep surfacing.

PROBLEM 01

DISCOM bottleneck

The scheme mandates a 30-day approval and commissioning window. The reality, per IEEFA, is 45-120 days. The hold-up is consistently at the DISCOM: technical feasibility checks, transformer capacity validation, net-meter installation, and the commissioning report that triggers subsidy disbursement. Most consumers think installation = done. It isn't.

PROBLEM 02

Net meter shortages

The bidirectional meters that make rooftop solar work are not always available when the household is ready. Smart-meter procurement and installation have lagged sanctions across most states — only ~32 million of 224 million sanctioned smart meters were installed by mid-2025. Without the meter, the system cannot be commissioned and the subsidy cannot be released.

PROBLEM 03

Net metering rules being rewritten

Maharashtra and Karnataka have already capped net-metering credits and introduced time-of-day tariffs. The reason: as more affluent households install rooftop solar and reduce their grid draw, the DISCOM's cross-subsidy pool — which lets it charge commercial and industrial users to subsidise residential — shrinks. Industry calls this risk the "utility death spiral".

The equity question

PM Surya Ghar subsidises the rooftop. It does not subsidise the rented apartment, the informal settlement, or the household without an Aadhaar-linked bank account. As affluent solar households reduce their grid draw but stay connected for evening backup, non-adopters — usually low-income families — face rising fixed charges to keep the wires running. A Down To Earth analysis published in April 2026 warned that without cross-subsidy modelling, regulators may face politically toxic tariff shocks. The scheme is real and works; the equity audit isn't being done yet.

05 / DISTRIBUTION

The wires still bleed.

Distribution companies (DISCOMs) are the part of the system most consumers actually interact with — the entity that sends the bill, runs the substation, handles outages. They are also the system's weakest link financially. Total DISCOM debt was approximately ₹7.26 lakh crore in late 2025. AT&C (Aggregate Technical & Commercial) losses — power that is generated and delivered but never paid for — have improved meaningfully but remain far above world averages.

The state-by-state picture is sharply uneven. Per the Power Finance Corporation's FY24 report, Karnataka, Uttar Pradesh, and Telangana together account for the bulk of DISCOM losses. Gujarat and Delhi are the standouts that actually post profits. Several states still record AT&C losses above 25%, and some exceed 35%. Where losses are high, two things almost always follow: load-shedding (planned power cuts during peak hours because the DISCOM cannot afford expensive power), and political pressure to under-tariff agricultural and residential users.

What you experience as a consumer

The visible failures of distribution are predictable and consistent across the country: local transformer overloads in summer evenings, especially in dense urban colonies where AC penetration outran the substation; billing disputes from the mismatch between consumer records and DISCOM databases when applying for solar or new connections; delayed subsidy disbursement for everything from PM-KUSUM pumps to rooftop solar; and localised load-shedding that varies by feeder rather than by district, because some feeders carry more agricultural pumping load than the system can fund.

Why the cycle persists

Political tariffs (free or near-free electricity for farmers in several states) starve DISCOMs of revenue. State subsidy reimbursements are often late, sometimes by years. DISCOMs then defer capex on transformers, conductors, and meters. Old infrastructure produces higher technical losses. Higher losses force regulators to allow higher tariffs for paying customers, which in turn increases the political pressure to subsidise. The Economic Survey 2025-26 flagged this directly — calling rising state-level revenue deficits and untargeted cash transfers a drag on power-sector capex.

06 / THE FIX

Batteries, finally.

The clearest answer to the duck curve is to move midday solar to evening demand. That means storage. India's installed grid-scale battery storage was under 1 GWh entering 2026. It is on track for ~5 GWh by year-end — a ten-fold jump in twelve months — and 346 GWh by 2033 under the base case (544 GWh if policy momentum holds). The Central Electricity Authority estimates the actual need by 2031-32 at 411 GWh, split between batteries and pumped hydro.

The economics finally work because two things happened in 2024-25. Global battery cell costs collapsed — China overbuilt manufacturing capacity, oversupplying the world by roughly 4x; turnkey BESS prices dropped about 40% in a single year. And the Indian government layered three policy pillars on top of falling costs: ₹91 billion of Viability Gap Funding covering up to 40% of capital costs on 43.2 GWh of standalone BESS; an Inter-State Transmission System (ISTS) charge waiver for storage co-located with renewables; and a Production-Linked Incentive scheme for 50 GWh of advanced chemistry cell manufacturing inside India.

"Solar peaks between 10 AM and 3 PM. Wind blows hardest at night and in specific corridors. Demand peaks in the evening, between 6 PM and 10 PM, precisely when the sun has set and the grid is leaning on coal plants cycling inefficiently to compensate. 2026 is the year the BESS sector in India crossed the line from emerging technology to mainstream grid asset."

— India Energy Storage Alliance · IESA-CES whitepaper, March 2026

Pumped hydro — older, less glamorous, but cheaper for longer duration — is being built in parallel. The 1 GW Tehri facility commissioned its first variable-speed unit in June 2025. Karnataka's 2 GW Sharavathy project is under development. India's total pumped-storage technical potential is roughly 176 GW; the CEA has mapped 51 GW to be developed by 2032.

07 / WHAT'S NEXT

The next three years.

The schemes and rules below are either operational, sanctioned, or in advanced consultation. They are the levers the government is pulling — in roughly chronological order of when their effects will land on the grid and on consumer bills.

FY 2026-27 · CURRENT
RDSS smart meter push
The Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (₹3 lakh crore, extended to March 2028) is the central plank for fixing the DISCOM side. Of 224 million smart meters sanctioned, roughly 32 million are installed; the remaining ~190 million land over the next two years. Smart meters end estimation-based billing, cut theft, and enable time-of-day tariffs.
FY 2026-27 · CURRENT
PM Surya Ghar — 1 crore homes target
₹22,000 crore allocated in Budget 2026-27 (up ~10% on FY26). Utility-led aggregation model expected to add another 10 lakh installations by March 2026. National Portal grievance redressal still weak; states are independently capping net-metering credits.
FY 2026-27 · CURRENT
Storage Obligations rise
DISCOMs must source a rising share of supply from storage-backed contracts: 1% (FY24) → 3% (FY27) → 4% (FY30). MNRE has mandated co-located 2-hour storage with new solar tenders. Energy Storage Obligation is the policy that puts batteries into the procurement pipeline by force, not just by preference.
2026-2028
National Electricity Policy 2026 ratified
Draft NEP 2026 explicitly aims for "24×7 supply" reliability — recognising that "hours matter, not just kilowatt-hours." Time-based procurement (round-the-clock and peak-hour tenders) replaces simple capacity tenders. Expect the language of the grid to shift from "capacity added" to "energy delivered when needed".
2026-2028
Time-of-Day tariffs nationwide
Following smart-meter deployment, time-of-day pricing — cheaper midday, more expensive evening — will be progressively rolled out for residential and small-commercial users in most states. This is the demand-side answer to the duck curve: nudge EV charging, washing machines, and water heaters to run between 11 AM and 4 PM.
2026-2030
AC minimum-temperature standards
Under active consideration: capping AC thermostat settings between 20°C and 28°C, mandatory inverter-class AC sales, tighter star-rating norms. Could deliver up to 60 GW of peak-demand savings by 2035 — the cheapest infrastructure India can build is the kilowatt-hour it doesn't have to generate.
2027-2030
500 GW non-fossil capacity
Officially the headline target — and India crossed 50% non-fossil installed capacity in 2025, five years ahead of the Paris pledge. But the relevant question is no longer capacity; it is dispatchable capacity. Expect the policy conversation to keep migrating from "how many gigawatts" to "how many gigawatt-hours, and at what time of day".
2030-2032
Storage build-out at scale
CEA target: 74 GW / 411 GWh of storage by FY32 (47 GW batteries, 27 GW pumped hydro). IESA base-case projection: 346 GWh of installed BESS by 2033. Bullish case: 544 GWh. This is the single most important number to watch — it determines whether the duck curve becomes a footnote or a permanent feature of Indian electricity.
2030-2070
Nuclear opens to private players
Budget 2026-27 confirmed nuclear power generation is being opened to private capital for the first time. Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) are the focus. Nuclear is the only dispatchable, firm, clean power source that scales — the bet is that 5-10 GW of SMR capacity by the mid-2030s will provide the "always on" baseload that pure renewables-plus-storage struggle to match in the longest evening peaks of summer.

The Grid is a technical reader on the operational reality of India's electricity system in 2026 — synthesised from CEA, CERC, IEA, MNRE, PFC, NITI Aayog, IEEFA, CEEW, IESA, and the Economic Survey 2025-26.
