The core paradox of 2025

"India's strongest macroeconomic performance in decades has collided with a global system that no longer rewards macroeconomic success with currency stability, capital inflows, or strategic insulation."

— V. Anantha Nageswaran, Chief Economic Adviser, in the Preface to the Survey

Why this matters to ordinary Indians

For households. Inflation at 1.7% means your monthly grocery bill rose almost nothing. Vegetable prices actually fell. The February 2025 budget raised the no-tax income threshold to ₹12 lakh — a family with two earners can now make up to roughly ₹26.7 lakh tax-free. In September, GST slabs were collapsed from many tiers down to two, with most items moving to the lower slab.

For workers. Four labour codes — compressing 29 central laws — were finally notified in November 2025. Gig and platform workers are now formally recognised, with provisions to bring them under social security. Unemployment fell to 4.7% in Q2 FY26 (CWS basis).

For business. Capacity utilisation in manufacturing is above its long-run average. The repo rate is down 125 basis points. A cash reserve ratio cut released roughly ₹2.5 lakh crore in fresh liquidity. The insurance sector opened to 100% foreign direct investment. Nuclear power generation opened to private players.

For India's standing. S&P upgraded India's sovereign rating from BBB– to BBB in August 2025 — the country's first upgrade from a major agency in nearly two decades. Morningstar DBRS and R&I followed.

Frame · The CEA's Three Global Scenarios for 2026

Three futures for the world. India needs buffers for all of them.

Rather than a single point forecast, this year's Survey opens with a scenario framework — an unusual move that reflects how uncertain the global picture has become. Each scenario has consequences for the rupee, capital flows, and India's export earnings.

40–45%
Business as in 2025 — but more fragile
The world muddles through. Trade frictions and financial stress episodes don't trigger systemic collapse, but margins of safety are thinner. Governments intervene more often to stabilise expectations. "Managed disorder."
40–45%
Disorderly multipolar breakdown
Strategic rivalry intensifies. Russia–Ukraine remains unresolved. Trade becomes explicitly coercive, sanctions proliferate, supply chains realign under political pressure. Sharper trade-offs between autonomy, growth, and stability.
10–20%
Reset toward limited cooperation
An off-ramp emerges. Major powers find a framework — even if narrow — to manage rivalry. Capital flows stabilise. Inflation eases more uniformly. The smallest probability, but the only one in which a "marathon-as-sprint" effort becomes optional.
In plain English

What this means: rough seas, possibly for years, not months.

The CEA's argument is that all three scenarios threaten capital flows into India — only the size and duration differ. Because India runs a goods trade deficit, it needs foreign capital and services exports to fund the bill. If that capital gets jittery, the rupee weakens. The policy answer he proposes: build buffers (reserves, supply diversification, payment routes), keep policy predictable, and run "a marathon as if it were a sprint."

6 min readState of the Economy · Pushing the growth frontier

Growth held up. The world expected less.

When the United States announced reciprocal tariffs in April 2025, then an additional 25% penal tariff on most Indian merchandise in August, every major forecaster downgraded India. Growth, in reality, accelerated. The First Advance Estimates place real GDP growth for FY26 at 7.4% — the fastest among major economies, for the fourth year running.

Private consumption: the largest single force

The share of private final consumption in GDP is now 61.5% — the highest since FY12. Consumption grew 7.5% in the first half of FY26, the fastest H1 reading since FY24 and well above the pre-Covid trend of 6.9%. Rural demand has stayed strong, helped by good monsoon and rising farm incomes; urban demand has picked up after the tax breaks of February and GST rationalisation in September.

The NABARD Rural Survey of November 2025 found that 79.2% of rural households reported higher consumption over the year — and that the share of monthly income spent on consumption rose to about 67%, the highest since the survey began.

Investment: a private cycle starting up

Gross fixed capital formation grew 7.6% in H1, above the pre-pandemic average. Public capex rose 28% year-on-year between April and November 2025. More striking is private intent: corporate investment announcements totalled ₹14.6 lakh crore in H1 FY26 versus ₹7.9 lakh crore a year earlier — and even higher than the previous decadal peak of ₹11.4 lakh crore in H1 FY24.

Capital goods imports are up double digits. Capacity utilisation in manufacturing is above its long-run average. These are the signs of a real investment cycle, not just public spending.

In plain English

Why "potential growth" being raised to 7% matters

Potential growth is the speed your economy can grow without overheating — without inflation taking off or current accounts blowing out. Three years ago the Survey set it at 6.5%. The fact that the same team has now revised it up half a percentage point is their way of saying: the reform momentum of recent years, capex by both Centre and states, the PLI schemes, the doubling of the airport network, faster freight on inland waterways, deeper financialisation — these have permanently lifted what the Indian economy can do. The Survey projects FY27 growth in the range of 6.8–7.2%.

5 min readFiscal Developments · Anchoring stability through credible consolidation

A deficit cut in half. And the bond market noticed.

In 2021, after Covid, India's Union fiscal deficit was 9.2% of GDP — a peacetime record. The Centre promised to halve it by FY26. In FY25 it came in at 4.8% (better than the 4.9% target), with FY26 budgeted at 4.4%. Three rating agencies upgraded India in 2025: Morningstar DBRS in May, S&P in August (BBB– → BBB, first major upgrade in two decades), and R&I in September.

India's fiscal deficit: from 9.2% of GDP to 4.4%, in five years

Source: Union Budget Documents, Controller General of Accounts · PA = Provisional Accounts, BE = Budget Estimates

What changed the trajectory

Revenue side: more tax, more cleanly

Centre's revenue receipts rose from about 8.5% of GDP (pre-pandemic average) to roughly 9.1% of GDP (post-pandemic). Direct taxes drove the bulk of this, helped by digital compliance, formalisation, and rising corporate profits. GST collections hit multiple all-time monthly highs in FY26, even as rates were cut.

Spending: less on revenue, more on capex

Revenue expenditure fell from 13.6% of GDP in FY22 to 10.9% in FY25. That created room for capital outlays. Capital expenditure rose from a pre-pandemic average of 1.7% of GDP to roughly 3% post-pandemic; effective capex (including grants for capital assets to states) reached 4% of GDP in FY25.

The state-level worry

The Survey devotes unusual space to state finances. Many states are running rising revenue deficits, driven by an expansion of unconditional cash transfers. The CEA's argument: these cash transfers crowd out capital investments — including in human capital. Worse, with Indian government bonds now globally indexed, investors increasingly look at general government finances, not just the Centre.

India's 10-year bond yields 6.7%; Indonesia's, with the same BBB rating, yields 6.3%. The CEA suggests state-level fiscal laxity may be lifting sovereign borrowing costs.

In plain English

Why "quality of expenditure" is the real story

A government can hit a fiscal deficit target by simply not spending — that's bad. India did the opposite: it kept spending, but rebalanced sharply toward things that create future capacity (roads, ports, defence, digital public infrastructure) and away from things that simply pay current bills (subsidies, salaries). Capex worth ₹11.21 lakh crore is budgeted for FY26 alone — nearly 4× the level five years ago.

4 min readMonetary Management & Financial Intermediation

The RBI cut. Aggressively.

In a single calendar year, the Reserve Bank of India cut the repo rate by 125 basis points and the cash reserve ratio by 100 basis points. Few major central banks moved that decisively. The result: roughly ₹2.5 lakh crore of fresh liquidity in the banking system, and a clear pass-through to lending rates. The MPC's stance was changed from accommodative to neutral in June 2025, giving room to respond either way.

Banks: the healthiest in a generation

Gross non-performing assets, which once consumed an entire policy debate during the 2014–18 "twin balance sheet" crisis, have fallen to multi-decade lows. Profit after tax across scheduled commercial banks is rising. Capital adequacy is comfortably above regulatory minimums. The faster transmission of policy rate cuts to lending rates means RBI's easing is actually reaching businesses.

A deeper structural argument: the cost of capital

The chapter makes an unusual point: India's high cost of capital isn't just about RBI policy rates. A country that persistently runs a current account deficit has to pay a risk premium to foreign savers. Only economies that consistently generate external surpluses — Germany, Japan, Korea, China at its peak — can finance investment cheaply at home. The implication: becoming a manufacturing exporter is, in the long run, the only path to a durably low cost of capital.

The structural insight

"India's long-run challenge is not merely to manage liquidity or credit cycles, but to transform itself into a surplus-generating economy. Only then can its cost of capital fall durably."

— Chapter 3, on why monetary easing alone cannot make capital cheap

6 min readExternal Sector · Playing the long game

Tariffs hit. Services held.

In April 2025 the US announced a 25% reciprocal tariff on Indian merchandise. In August it added a 25% penal tariff on top of that. By any rational forecast, India's exports should have buckled. They didn't — merchandise exports still grew 2.4% in the April–December period, and services exports grew 6.5%. Total exports for FY25 hit a record USD 825.3 billion.

India's export composition — services have done the heavy lifting

Source: RBI / DGCIS · 5-year CAGR by category, FY20–FY25

Why services exports are not enough

India is now the world's 7th largest exporter of services. Its share of global services trade has more than doubled from 2% in 2005 to 4.3% in 2024. IT and IT-enabled services, business services and the rise of Global Capability Centres are the engine. Over the five years since FY20, total exports grew at a compounded 9.4% — but merchandise grew only 6.4%.

Yet the Survey is blunt about why services are not a substitute for manufacturing: services firms can bypass weak institutions, relocate easily, and put little pressure on the state to reform. They generate forex but do not, by themselves, force the kind of state-capacity upgrade that produces a strong currency. "Currency strength, in general, or currency stability during crises, has always eluded countries that could not become successful and significant exporters of manufactured goods."

India's balance of payments components — services and remittances offset goods deficit

Source: RBI Handbook of Statistics · Quarterly, USD billion

The rupee question

Between 1 April 2025 and 22 January 2026 the rupee depreciated about 6.5% against the dollar. The Survey calls the rupee "punching below its weight" — it does not reflect India's strong macro fundamentals. Why? A wider balance-of-payments deficit in H1 FY26 (USD 6.4 billion versus a surplus of USD 23.8 billion a year earlier), tepid foreign portfolio flows, and global capital being sucked into AI investments in the US, Taiwan, and Korea.

India's new trade diplomacy

2025 saw a slew of trade agreements: with the UK, Oman, New Zealand, and a comprehensive FTA with the European Union after three years of negotiation (pending EU Parliament ratification). The EU FTA is the largest: it widens market access for India's labour-intensive manufacturing — textiles, leather, marine products — and enables deeper technology integration. Trade talks with the US continue.

In plain English

Why "11 months of import cover" should reassure you

Forex reserves are India's insurance policy. As of January 16, 2026, India's reserves can cover 11 months of imports and around 94% of all external debt. For comparison: the textbook adequate threshold is 3 months. India has built a war chest. When the rupee did weaken in 2025, the depreciation was orderly precisely because of this cushion.

5 min readInflation · Tamed and Anchored

Inflation at 1.7%. Vegetable prices actually fell.

The Consumer Price Index averaged 1.7% in April–December 2025 — the lowest in the current series. October 2025's reading of 0.3% was the lowest monthly print on record. Food inflation went outright negative from June 2025. This happened alongside 8% real GDP growth in H1 — disinflation without contraction, an unusual combination.

Where the disinflation came from — food, especially vegetables

The "sticky core" mirage

Headline inflation collapsed but core inflation (excluding food and fuel) appeared stuck around 4.6%. The Survey shows this is misleading: core ex-gold-and-silver decelerated from 3.4% to 2.3% between June and December. The "wedge" — about 235 basis points — is almost entirely the surge in precious metals prices driven by global safe-haven demand, not by underlying domestic pressure. Strip gold and silver and core inflation is also falling.

An echo from 2008–14

It's worth remembering the contrast: between 2008 and 2014, India's CPI hit double digits repeatedly. The S&P rating action explicitly noted: "In the past three years, despite volatility in global energy prices and supply-side shocks, CPI growth averaged 5.5 per cent. In recent months, it stayed at the lower bound of the RBI's target range of 2–6 per cent." Inflation targeting, introduced in 2016, has delivered.

In plain English

The growth–inflation chart in one image

Among the world's major emerging economies, India is the only one that combines ~8% real growth in H1 FY26 with ~2.8% inflation (calendar 2025 IMF basis). Brazil grew slowly and inflated. Russia grew slowly under sanctions and inflated harder. China grew faster but slipped into outright deflation. India hit the rare quadrant: fast growth, contained prices.

4 min readAgriculture & Food Management · Raising productivity, securing income, ensuring food security

Allied sectors are doing the work. Crops are stuck.

Agriculture and allied sectors contribute about a fifth of national income — and employ 46.1% of India's workforce. Over the decade FY16–FY25, the sector's average growth of 4.45% is the highest of any decade in 50 years. But the headline hides the real story: livestock grew 7.1% annually, fisheries 8.8%, while the crop sector managed just 3.5%.

Agricultural growth by decade — the best ten years in fifty

Source: Ramesh Chand presidential address, 24th IASSI Annual Conference, October 2025 · Decadal averages

The structural shift

Livestock GVA grew nearly 195% between FY15 and FY24 — a compound annual growth rate of 12.77%. Fisheries production rose more than 140% between 2014 and 2025. India is now the world's third-largest fish producer.

Crops, by contrast, remain locked in volatility tied to monsoons and stuck around 3.5% trend growth. Fragmented landholdings, limited mechanisation, water stress in key cropping zones, and modest productivity gains continue to bind.

The policy stack

  • PM-KISAN direct income support to farmers
  • MSP at 1.5× cost of production for major crops since 2018–19
  • Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchai Yojana for irrigation expansion
  • e-NAM electronic agricultural marketplace
  • Agriculture Infrastructure Fund for post-harvest infrastructure
  • FPOs (Farmer Producer Organisations) for collective bargaining
  • Digital Agriculture Mission for tech adoption
In plain English

Why this still matters for everyone, not just farmers

Half of India still works in agriculture. When rural incomes go up, FMCG sales go up, two-wheeler sales go up, the demand for everything from cement to consumer electronics goes up. The NABARD survey of November 2025 reported 79% of rural households consumed more — that one statistic alone is the most reliable signal of broad-based growth.

Half the economy. Largest single FDI recipient.

Services contribute more than half of India's Gross Value Added and grew 9.1% in FY26 — up from 7.2% in FY25. India is unusual among developing economies in having grown via services at a relatively low income level. The sector has averaged 7–8% growth annually for years, with a standard deviation lower than industry, manufacturing or even overall GDP — the closest thing the Indian economy has to a stable backbone.

5 min readIndustry's Next Leap · Structural transformation and global integration

Manufacturing snapped back. And went high-tech.

Manufacturing GVA grew 8.4% in H1 FY26 — up from 4.8% a year earlier. Real industry GVA growth picked up to 7% (versus 5.9% in FY25). But the more interesting number is composition: medium- and high-technology activities now account for 46.3% of India's total manufacturing value added. This is a different economy from the textiles-and-steel India of a decade ago.

India is among the fastest-growing industrial economies in Q3 2025

Source: UNIDO World Manufacturing Production and Trade Quarterly · QoQ manufacturing growth, Q3 2025

PLI: the policy that's working

The Production Linked Incentive scheme — rolled out across 14 sectors since 2020 — has tied government incentives to actual production. Electronics has been the most dramatic case: smartphone exports rose from negligible levels to becoming one of India's top export categories. iPhone manufacturing has moved meaningfully to Tamil Nadu and Karnataka.

India's Competitive Industrial Performance ranking improved to 37th in 2023, up from 40th in 2022 (UNIDO).

The CEA's warning on upstream protection

The chapter argues that protection for upstream industries — textile fibres, steel, aluminium — has gone too far. Tariff walls in upstream sectors function as a tax on downstream exporters. The East Asian lesson, the Survey reminds: upstream industrialisation succeeds only when disciplined by global competition. "Decisions about what not to protect can be as important as decisions about what to support."

Five pillars for Viksit Bharat @2047

Ease of Doing Business · R&D and Innovation · Skilling · Infrastructure & Logistics · Scaling MSMEs

The Survey lists these as the policy pillars for India's industrial leap — with the launch of the Anusandhan National Research Foundation (ANRF) and the new Research, Development & Innovation Fund as concrete steps to crowd in private R&D.

4 min readInvestment & Infrastructure · Strengthening connectivity, capacity, competitiveness

Capex up nearly 89% in four years.

The Centre's capital outlay rose from ₹5.92 lakh crore in FY22 to ₹11.21 lakh crore budgeted for FY26 — a 89% jump in four years. Studies put the multiplier of public infrastructure spend at 2.5 to 3.5× GDP over the medium term. That means every rupee spent on roads, ports, railways, airports, and digital infrastructure has produced ₹2.5–₹3.5 of additional output. It is the single largest reason FY26 growth surprised to the upside.

Centre's capital expenditure has nearly doubled in five years

Source: Union Budget Documents · ₹ lakh crore, BE = Budget Estimates

The headline numbers behind the chart

  • Airport network has doubled over the past decade, easing logistics and tourism constraints.
  • Inland waterways freight growing rapidly — a cheaper, lower-emission alternative to road and rail for bulk goods.
  • BharatNet, 5G rollout, UPI, Aadhaar, FASTag, DigiYatra, ULIP — the Digital Public Infrastructure stack underpinning everything from payments to logistics planning.
  • RBI Project Finance Directions 2025 (effective 1 October 2025) — unified framework for infrastructure lending across all financial institutions.
  • SEBI's SM REIT framework reduces minimum asset size from ₹500 crore to ₹50 crore — opens up smaller real estate assets to retail-accessible structures.
  • InvITs and REITs raised ₹13,893 crore between April and November 2025 alone — long-term institutional capital flowing into infrastructure assets.
In plain English

Why "PM GatiShakti" became the quiet revolution

Launched in 2021, PM GatiShakti is essentially a single, GIS-enabled platform on which every ministry, every state, and now even private developers can see infrastructure plans and existing assets. It sounds bureaucratic; in practice it has meant fewer roads being dug up by the gas pipeline contractor a month after they were paved. Multimodal coordination — once an Indian punchline — is becoming routine. The chapter argues this is what is now turning capex into growth multiplier rather than waste.

5 min readEnvironment & Climate Change · Building a resilient, competitive, development-driven India

"Development is itself a form of adaptation."

This year's climate chapter is unusually candid. It takes a hard look at Europe's transition — the Netherlands' grid congestion, Spain's April 2025 blackout — and draws a lesson: transitions that outpace institutional capacity become fragile, not green. The Survey makes the case for sequencing: building grid, storage, and balancing capacity before compressing the renewables timeline. It is one of the most policy-substantive chapters in the document.

India's actual energy mix challenge

Achieving sustained growth requires substantial expansion of affordable, reliable electricity. Renewables will play a growing role, but capacity additions don't automatically translate into dependable supply during non-solar peak hours. As renewable penetration rises, the cost of maintaining dispatchable thermal capacity rises too — even as that thermal utilisation declines. Grid stability becomes the binding constraint, not capacity.

The Survey's recommendation: maintain sufficient dispatchable thermal capacity alongside renewables expansion, and treat climate strategy as an energy system strategy rather than a standalone target.

CBAM is the new tariff

The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, fully effective from 2026, levies tariffs on imports of carbon-intensive goods like steel and cement. India is a target. The chapter argues for capacity-building in carbon measurement, accounting standards, and bilateral mutual recognition — not for capitulating to one country's methodology applied unilaterally.

India's climate strategy pillars

  • Adaptation — climate-resilient agriculture, water security, urban resilience
  • Mitigation — renewables, battery storage, nuclear expansion
  • Mission LiFE — behavioural change at citizen level
  • Climate finance — pushing for multilateral institution reform
"Development is, in itself, a form of adaptation. While emissions mitigation is important, effective climate action depends on improving societal capacity to withstand shocks through stronger health systems, climate-resilient infrastructure, enhanced agricultural productivity, and access to affordable and reliable energy." — Chapter 10, on the philosophy of India's climate strategy

5 min readEducation & Health · What works and what's next

24.7 crore students. Now the harder problem: quality.

India runs one of the largest school systems in the world: 24.69 crore students across 14.71 lakh schools, supported by 1.01 crore teachers. Gross Enrolment Ratio is 95.4% at preparatory level. The job of getting children into school is largely done. The job of making sure they learn — which the NEP 2020 calls Foundational Literacy and Numeracy — is the unfinished agenda.

India's education gap: Expected Years of Schooling vs peers

Source: UNDP Human Development Report 2025

Health: new frontiers, old foundations

Life expectancy is up. Infant and maternal mortality continue to fall. Ayushman Bharat-PMJAY covers more than 50 crore people for hospitalisation up to ₹5 lakh per family per year. But the next health battles are different.

The chapter spotlights obesity, non-communicable diseases (cardiovascular, diabetes), and the psychological effects of digital addiction — particularly in productive age groups. These are middle-class diseases of plenty, and they are arriving fast. Prevention, not treatment, is the policy question.

A flagship school programme

The PM SHRI scheme has established 13,076 model schools across 33 states and UTs, with NEP-aligned features — ICT classrooms, integrated science labs, sports infrastructure, eco-clubs, and "Bagless Days" for experiential learning.

Anganwadi co-location: 2,99,544 schools (32.9%) now host Anganwadi Centres on premises, bringing 3+ year olds into the educational ecosystem.

The APAAR (Automated Permanent Academic Account Registry) system uses Aadhaar to track student progress digitally across school, higher and skill education.

5 min readEmployment & Skill Development · Getting skilling right

56.2 crore working. And four labour codes — finally — in force.

A total of 56.2 crore people aged 15+ were employed in Q2 FY26 (July–September). The quarter added 8.7 lakh new jobs over Q1. Unemployment in Q2 FY26 (current weekly status basis) was 4.7%. India's demographic dividend will peak around 2030, when nearly 65% of the population will be in the 15–59 age group.

Source: PLFS Monthly & Quarterly Bulletin, MoSPI · CWS basis, age 15+

The Labour Codes, decoded

In November 2025, the central government notified the four Labour Codes — a reform that had been on paper since 2019. These consolidate 29 central labour laws into four codes covering wages, industrial relations, social security, and occupational safety. Critically, gig and platform workers are now formally recognised and can be registered for social security schemes.

For employers, the codes simplify compliance and offer more flexibility. For workers, they extend social security to a vastly broader section of the workforce. This is the most significant labour market reform since the 1990s.

The female participation puzzle

A 2024 Time Use Survey by MoSPI revealed: women aged 15–59 spend on average 363 minutes a day on unpaid activities, men only 123. Women's participation in regular wage jobs in rural areas is just 10.8%. The majority of working women are own-account workers (37.5%) or helpers in household enterprises (34.2%) — flexible work that fits the caregiving burden.

Policy responses being scaled: safe affordable accommodation for women workers, flexible/hybrid work arrangements, and creches near workplaces.

In plain English

The gig worker question, settled (for now)

By some estimates, India will have 23.5 million gig workers by 2029–30. The Code on Social Security 2020 (now operational) requires platforms like Zomato, Swiggy, Ola and Uber to contribute toward a social security fund for gig workers — for benefits like accident cover, maternity benefits, and old-age protection. Implementation rules are expected to be in place over the next few months. India is among the few countries to legally bring platform workers under a national social security architecture.

4 min readRural Development & Social Progress · From participation to partnership

Poverty cut by three-quarters in two decades.

In June 2025, the World Bank revised its International Poverty Line from USD 2.15 to USD 3.00 per day (PPP 2021 prices). On the new line, India's extreme poverty rate for 2022–23 stands at 5.3%, with lower-middle-income poverty at 23.9%. The Multidimensional Poverty Index — measuring education, health and living standards — has collapsed from 55.3% in 2005–06 to 11.28% in 2022–23.

Multidimensional poverty: from 55% to 11% in 17 years

Source: NITI Aayog Multidimensional Poverty Index · NFHS-3 (2005–06), NFHS-5 (2019–21), HCES (2022–23)

The state-led innovations

Bihar's Satat Jeevikoparjan Yojana (SJY) uses a "graduation approach" for ultra-poor women — asset transfers, training, mentoring over 24 months. A J-PAL study found beneficiaries had stable income even when casual labour collapsed during Covid.

Kerala's poverty eradication model identifies vulnerable households through community participation led by local governments, ASHA workers, and the Kudumbashree network. Individualised micro-plans are tracked digitally.

The Union's response: Samaveshi Aajeevika Yojana under DAY-NRLM, scaling the graduation approach nationally.

Rural infrastructure as a poverty exit

The decade saw an extraordinary rural infrastructure build-out:

  • Jal Jeevan Mission — taking piped drinking water to every rural household
  • PM Awas Yojana — Grameen — pucca houses for the rural poor
  • PMGSY — rural connectivity with all-weather roads
  • Swachh Bharat Mission — household toilet construction at unprecedented scale
  • Saubhagya — household electrification
  • Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana — LPG connections for rural women

This is the infrastructure that turned poverty reduction from a transfer-payment story to a service-delivery story.

6 min readEvolution of the AI Ecosystem in India · The way forward

India's AI bet: not scale. Sectors.

McKinsey's 2025 State of AI survey: 88% of organisations globally use AI in at least one business function, up from 50% in 2022. 31% are scaling it. 7% have fully deployed. The Survey's view is that India should not try to compete head-on with US hyperscalers on foundation model scale. Instead: sector-specific AI applications, open and interoperable systems, data sovereignty for Indian users.

AI adoption has nearly doubled in three years

Source: McKinsey State of AI Report 2025 · % of surveyed organisations using AI in at least one function

The strategic logic

Why not chase the frontier

Training cutting-edge foundation models requires extraordinary capital, compute, energy, and specialised hardware. Power-hungry data centres are concentrated in a handful of locations globally. Of the top 10 highest-value greenfield projects in 2024, four were in semiconductors and three of those in the US. The Survey is realistic: India will not out-spend the US, China, or even Korea on the foundation-model layer.

But the application layer is wide open — and India has structural advantages: large diverse datasets across languages, deep IT services bench, public digital infrastructure (Aadhaar, UPI, ONDC), and a young labour force.

The labour question

Early evidence from a Yale Budget Lab study and from Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (2025) suggests AI has not produced large-scale labour displacement in the US so far. But the Survey's own analysis of US Professional, Business and Information Services data finds something subtle: while employment levels are not falling, the marginal responsiveness of employment to output growth has weakened in the post-GenAI era. In plain terms, output is growing faster than jobs.

The policy implication for India: monitor closely, prepare for adaptive reskilling, but don't panic. The biggest near-term opportunity for AI in India is in complementing labour at scale — across agriculture, health diagnostics, education, government service delivery.

India's AI architecture in three principles

1. Bottom-up, sector-specific over top-down scale.
2. Open, interoperable systems over closed proprietary stacks.
3. Sequencing — enable experimentation first, scale next, regulate the bindingly risky last.

The chapter explicitly rejects "unsustainable models adopted in advanced economies" as templates. Data governance is built around the principle that value from Indian data must accrue to Indians.

5 min readUrbanisation · Making India's cities work for its citizens

Cities aren't habitats. They're economic infrastructure.

A meta-analysis by Grover, Lall & Timmis (World Bank, 2021) found that doubling city size in India typically boosts productivity by 12%. Yet India's urbanisation has paradoxically slowed. The proportion of Indians living in urban areas is now marginally below the average for lower-middle-income countries. The chapter argues this isn't a feature; it's a bug — a result of fragmented metropolitan governance, restrictive zoning, and broken transit, not citizen preference.

The four binding constraints

  • Land — restricted density, unclear titles, limited land recycling. Floor space indices in Indian metros are a fraction of comparable Asian cities.
  • Housing — affordable housing is artificially scarce because density is artificially restricted
  • Mobility — transit systems overly reliant on private vehicles; metros are coming but urban buses have stagnated
  • Sanitation, waste, water — vast expansion in recent years; now the bar is reliability, circularity, and efficiency

The deeper institutional problem

Beneath these is a deeper issue: fragmented metropolitan governance and limited fiscal autonomy for cities. Mumbai cannot plan as Mumbai; it must coordinate across a half-dozen bodies. Bengaluru's Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike controls a fraction of what city-level governments do elsewhere. The Survey points to Elinor Ostrom's work on commons and Indian economist D. R. Gadgil: development outcomes depend on institutions and social organisation, not investment alone.

The intangible dimension — civic norms, respect for public space, collective behaviour — gets equal billing with infrastructure. Cities can be funded and still feel ungovernable.

In plain English

Why this chapter matters even if you live in a village

Over 70% of India's urban population lives in Class I cities (over 100,000 people). 52 metropolitan cities (over 1 million) account for 42.3% of all urban residents. The economic argument the Survey is making — cities are a form of critical economic infrastructure that drives national productivity — implies that fixing Mumbai, Bengaluru, Chennai, Delhi and a dozen others is, in effect, a national growth strategy. Bad cities are not just a Mumbai problem; they are an India productivity problem.

7 min readFrom Import Substitution to Strategic Resilience & Strategic Indispensability

The state must become entrepreneurial.

The final chapter (in two parts) is the Survey's intellectual peak. It frames India's transition in three steps: from import substitution (the old model — protect industry from imports) → to strategic resilience (absorb shocks, preserve stability) → to strategic indispensability (become so critical to global value chains that coercion becomes impossible). The vehicle for all three: a fundamentally upgraded state capacity.

"The foundation of well-being is dharma. The foundation of dharma is economic strength. And the foundation of economic strength is the state." — Chanakya Sutras, epigraph to Chapter 16

The three transitions, in one sentence each

Three strategic stances for India in a fragmenting world

Adapted from Chapter 16, Economic Survey 2025–26
01

Import Substitution — the old default

Build domestic capacity behind tariff walls. Works for some upstream sectors briefly. Fails because protected firms grow lazy and pass costs to downstream exporters. The 20th-century model India is leaving behind.

02

Strategic Resilience — where India is now

Absorb shocks. Preserve stability. Diversify trade partners, build reserves, secure essential supplies (semiconductors, critical minerals, defence inputs). The current state of play. Necessary but not sufficient.

03

Strategic Indispensability — the destination

Become a source of reliability, capability, and value others cannot easily replace. UPI exported as DPI. Semiconductors and rare earth processing built at scale. Pharmaceutical capability deepened. Indian standards adopted in emerging tech. The point at which coercion becomes too costly to attempt.

Part II · The entrepreneurial state

Borrowing from Mariana Mazzucato's framing, the Survey argues India's state needs to evolve from compliance-based administration to capability-based execution under uncertainty. Concretely:

  • Act before certainty emerges — mission-mode platforms in semiconductors and green hydrogen are early examples
  • Structure risk rather than avoid it — public procurement reformed to enable first-of-a-kind domestic innovation
  • Learn systematically from experimentation — state-level deregulation compacts replacing inspection-based control with trust-based compliance
  • Correct course without paralysis — Quality Control Orders that harmed downstream industries put on hold; not defended out of inertia

The Survey is unflinching about the diagnosis: bureaucratic risk aversion, fragmented accountability, and proceduralism weaken the State's ability to act decisively. The way out is not deregulation as withdrawal — but deregulation as institutional reorientation: shifting administrative effort from low-value policing toward coordination, facilitation, and problem-solving.

The "Catch-22" warning

"Joseph Heller's metaphor captured how systems, once built to ensure safety and order, can end up entrapping those they are meant to serve — where every rule is followed, yet the outcome grows steadily more irrational."

The CEA invokes Heller to make a specific point: India's challenge is not to design better policies, but to ensure that rules, incentives, and administrative reflexes serve national resilience rather than procedural self-preservation. Policy reforms matter. Process reforms matter arguably more.

In plain English

What this chapter is really saying

India has proven it can grow at 7%+. It has proven its banks are sound, its inflation is anchored, its fiscal house is in order. What it has not yet proven is that its state apparatus — its bureaucracy, its courts, its regulators, its state governments — can move fast enough, take risks intelligently enough, and learn from failure quickly enough to handle a world where geopolitics drives economics. The final 100 pages of the Survey are, in effect, a memo to the Indian state about the Indian state. That this kind of self-examination appears in an official government publication is itself a sign of the maturity of the policy conversation.
